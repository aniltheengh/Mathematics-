## Definition

A **group** $(G,\cdot)$ has an associative binary operation, identity $e$,
and inverse for every element. It is abelian if the operation commutes. A
subgroup $H\le G$ is a subset that itself forms a group under the same
operation. A cyclic group is generated by one element:
$\langle g\rangle=\{g^n:n\in\mathbb Z\}$. The symmetric group $S_n$ contains
all permutations of $n$ objects under composition; it has $n!$ elements.

A group homomorphism $\phi:G\to H$ preserves multiplication.
Its kernel is $\{g:\phi(g)=e_H\}$. A subgroup $N$ is normal if
$gNg^{-1}=N$ for every $g\in G$; only then does the coset set $G/N$ inherit
the group product $(gN)(hN)=ghN$ independently of representatives.

A **ring** has an abelian additive group, associative multiplication, a
multiplicative identity $1$, and both distributive laws. We allow noncommutative
rings but require homomorphisms to preserve $1$. For quotient rings the ideal
must be two-sided. An ideal is an additive subgroup stable under multiplication
by ring elements on both sides. A field is a commutative ring with $1\ne0$
in which every nonzero element is invertible. An integral domain is a
commutative nonzero ring with no zero divisors.

## Intuitive explanation

Abstract algebra records the laws that make a calculation valid, then asks
which conclusions use only those laws. Integers modulo a prime and real
numbers share field laws, even though one field is finite and the other is
infinite. A quotient deliberately identifies objects differing by a specified
substructure. Normality or the ideal condition is what makes the inherited
operation consistent; it is not optional terminology.

Groups describe reversible symmetries; rings combine addition and
multiplication; modules describe linear combinations over rings. Fields have
especially regular linear algebra because scalar division is possible.

## Mathematical notation

$gH=\{gh:h\in H\}$ is a left coset; $[G:H]$ counts left cosets.
$\ker\phi$ and $\operatorname{im}\phi$ are kernel and image. $R[x]$ is the
polynomial ring over $R$, $(f)$ is the ideal generated by $f$, and
$R/I$ is the quotient ring. For a field extension $K/F$, $[K:F]$ is the
dimension of $K$ as an $F$-vector space. $\operatorname{Gal}(K/F)$ denotes
field automorphisms of $K$ fixing $F$ pointwise.

## Fundamental properties

**Group structure.** A nonempty subset is a subgroup when $ab^{-1}$ remains
in it for every pair $a,b$ in it. Kernels are normal, and
$G/\ker\phi\cong\operatorname{im}\phi$ (first isomorphism theorem).
Every subgroup of a cyclic group is cyclic. For finite groups,
$|G|=[G:H]|H|$ (Lagrange's theorem), so every element's order divides $|G|$.
The converse “every divisor occurs as an element order” is false in general.

A group action on a set $X$ assigns $g\cdot x$ with
$e\cdot x=x$ and $(gh)\cdot x=g\cdot(h\cdot x)$.
The orbit is $G\cdot x$ and stabilizer is
$G_x=\{g:g\cdot x=x\}$. For finite $G$,
$|G\cdot x|=|G|/|G_x|$. For example the four rotations of a square act
transitively on its four vertices, and a chosen vertex has trivial stabilizer.

**Rings and fields.** In a commutative ring, an ideal $\mathfrak p\ne R$ is
prime if $ab\in\mathfrak p$ implies $a\in\mathfrak p$ or
$b\in\mathfrak p$; equivalently $R/\mathfrak p$ is a domain.
An ideal $\mathfrak m\ne R$ is maximal if there is no strictly intermediate
ideal; equivalently $R/\mathfrak m$ is a field. For a field $F$ and
nonconstant $f\in F[x]$, $F[x]/(f)$ is a field exactly when $f$ is
irreducible. Polynomial division supplies inverses via Bézout's identity.

An extension element $\alpha$ is algebraic over $F$ when a nonzero polynomial
in $F[x]$ annihilates it. Its monic irreducible minimal polynomial has degree
$[F(\alpha):F]$. A finite field has $p^r$ elements for prime $p$ and integer
$r\ge1$; for each prime power such a field exists and is unique up to
isomorphism. This classification is cited without proof.

## Important formulas and identities

For finite extensions $F\subseteq E\subseteq K$,

$$[K:F]=[K:E][E:F].$$

For a finite Galois extension $K/F$ (finite, normal, and separable),

$$|\operatorname{Gal}(K/F)|=[K:F],\qquad
E\longleftrightarrow\operatorname{Gal}(K/E).$$

The correspondence reverses inclusions between intermediate fields $E$ and
subgroups of $\operatorname{Gal}(K/F)$. Normal subgroups correspond to
intermediate fields Galois over $F$, with the associated quotient group.
This is the fundamental theorem of Galois theory, cited without proof; without
the Galois hypotheses the displayed correspondence need not hold.

A left $R$-module $M$ is an abelian group with scalar action satisfying
$r(m+n)=rm+rn$, $(r+s)m=rm+sm$, $(rs)m=r(sm)$, and $1m=m$.
Over a field this is a vector space. Over $\mathbb Z$ it is exactly an
abelian group. A representation of $G$ on a vector space $V$ over $F$ is a
homomorphism $\rho:G\to\operatorname{GL}(V)$. For $C_2=\{e,s\}$, over
$\mathbb R$ one example is

$$\rho(e)=I_2,\qquad\rho(s)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\rho(s)^2=I_2.$$

This decomposes into invariant horizontal and vertical one-dimensional
subspaces. Its trace, or character value at $s$, is $0$.

## Derivations and proofs

**Lagrange's theorem.** Two left cosets that intersect are equal: if
$gh_1=kh_2$, then $g=k(h_2h_1^{-1})$, giving $gH=kH$.
Every element lies in one coset, so these cosets partition $G$. Multiplication
by $g$ gives a bijection $H\to gH$, so all cosets have $|H|$ elements.
Counting the partition proves $|G|=[G:H]|H|$.

**The quotient by a kernel.** Define
$\overline\phi(g\ker\phi)=\phi(g)$. If two cosets agree, their
representatives differ by a kernel element and have equal images, so this
map is well-defined. It preserves products and is surjective onto the image.
If its image is the identity, the representative belongs to the kernel, so
the coset is the identity coset. Thus it is injective, proving the first
isomorphism theorem.

**Advanced commutative algebra.** In this paragraph assume rings are commutative with identity, and localization sets contain $1$ and exclude $0$. A ring is Noetherian if every increasing
chain of ideals stabilizes, equivalently every ideal is finitely generated.
Hilbert's basis theorem says $R[x]$ is Noetherian when $R$ is (cited without
proof). Localization $S^{-1}R$ inverts a multiplicatively closed set $S$.
For $\mathbb Z$ and prime $p$, taking denominators not divisible by $p$
gives $\mathbb Z_{(p)}$; its elements outside the ideal $p\mathbb Z_{(p)}$
are units, so it is a local ring with that unique maximal ideal.

**Advanced homological algebra.** A chain complex has homomorphisms
$d_n:C_n\to C_{n-1}$ with $d_{n-1}d_n=0$. Its homology

$$H_n=\ker d_n/\operatorname{im}d_{n+1}$$

measures failure of exactness. In the complex
$0\to\mathbb Z\xrightarrow{2}\mathbb Z\xrightarrow{0}\mathbb Z\to0$,
with these groups in degrees $2,1,0$, the degree-one homology is
$\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z$. Tensor products need not preserve injections:
tensoring the injection $\mathbb Z\xrightarrow{2}\mathbb Z$ with
$\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z$ gives the zero map between two copies of
$\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z$. A free resolution of $\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z$
then computes
$\operatorname{Tor}_1^{\mathbb Z}(\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z,\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z)
\cong\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z$. Thus the derived object records a concrete
failure of exactness rather than serving as a new name for ordinary division.

## Worked examples

**1. A quotient group.** The map $\phi:\mathbb Z\to\mathbb Z/5\mathbb Z$
takes an integer to its remainder class. It is additive and surjective, with
kernel $5\mathbb Z$. Thus $\mathbb Z/5\mathbb Z$ is exactly the quotient by
the multiples of $5$. Addition of classes $[3]+[4]=[7]=[2]$ is independent
of choosing representatives such as $8$ and $-1$.

**2. Constructing a four-element field.** Over $\mathbb F_2$, the polynomial
$f(t)=t^2+t+1$ has no root: both $f(0)$ and $f(1)$ are $1$.
A quadratic without a root is irreducible, so
$\mathbb F_4=\mathbb F_2[t]/(f)$ is a field.
Let $\alpha=[t]$. Then $\alpha^2=\alpha+1$, and
$\alpha(\alpha+1)=\alpha^2+\alpha=1$.
The four elements are $0,1,\alpha,\alpha+1$; the last two are mutual inverses.
This field differs from $\mathbb Z/4\mathbb Z$, where $[2]^2=0$.

**3. A field extension and its Galois group.** In
$K=\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)$, each element is $a+b\sqrt2$ with rational $a,b$.
The polynomial $x^2-2$ is irreducible over $\mathbb Q$, so $[K:\mathbb Q]=2$.
Any automorphism fixing $\mathbb Q$ sends $\sqrt2$ to a root of its minimal
polynomial, hence to $\pm\sqrt2$. Both choices give automorphisms, so
$\operatorname{Gal}(K/\mathbb Q)\cong C_2$. Its fixed elements under the
nonidentity map satisfy $a+b\sqrt2=a-b\sqrt2$, hence $b=0$; the fixed field
is exactly $\mathbb Q$.

**4. An algebraic curve as a ring.** Over an algebraically closed field $k$, take
$V=\{(x,y):y=x^2\}$. The substitution map
$k[x,y]\to k[t]$, $x\mapsto t,y\mapsto t^2$, is onto and has kernel
$(y-x^2)$, as polynomial division in $y$ shows. Therefore
$k[x,y]/(y-x^2)\cong k[t]$; the coordinate ring records polynomial functions
on the parabola in the standard algebraic setting. By contrast
$k[x,y]/(xy)$ has nonzero classes $[x],[y]$ with zero product, reflecting
the union of the two coordinate axes.

For an algebraically closed field $k$, Hilbert's Nullstellensatz says
$I(V(I))=\sqrt I$ for ideals of $k[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ (cited without proof).
The radical is necessary: $y^2=0$ and $y=0$ have the same ordinary point set
but different quotient rings. The prime spectrum $\operatorname{Spec}R$
extends the viewpoint from point sets to all prime ideals, enabling schemes;
scheme theory is not developed fully in this introduction.

## Practice problems

1. **ABS-01:** Find the order of $[8]$ in the additive group $\mathbb Z/12\mathbb Z$ and list its generated subgroup.
2. **ABS-02:** Explain why $\mathbb Z/9\mathbb Z$ is not a field, and identify the ideal corresponding to reduction modulo $3$.
3. **ABS-03:** Compute $(1+\alpha)^2$ and $\alpha^3$ in the four-element field above.
4. **ABS-04:** Is $\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z$ a free $\mathbb Z$-module? Justify using torsion.
5. **ABS-05:** For $C_2$ acting on $\mathbb R$ by $s\cdot x=-x$, find the orbit and stabilizer at $0$ and at $2$.
6. **ABS-06:** Compute all homology groups of the displayed three-term integer complex.

## Answers and explanations

1. The order is $12/\gcd(8,12)=3$. The subgroup is
$\{[0],[8],[4]\}$, since the next addition returns to $[0]$.
2. $[3]\ne[0]$ but $[3]^2=[0]$, so $[3]$ cannot have an inverse.
Reduction $\mathbb Z/9\mathbb Z\to\mathbb Z/3\mathbb Z$ has kernel
$\{[0],[3],[6]\}$, an ideal; the quotient by it is a field.
3. Characteristic two cancels the cross term:
$(1+\alpha)^2=1+\alpha^2=\alpha$.
Also $\alpha^3=\alpha(\alpha+1)=1$.
4. No. Its nonzero element is killed by multiplication by $2$.
A free $\mathbb Z$-module has no nonzero element killed by a nonzero integer,
because this would force every integer coefficient in a basis expansion to vanish.
5. At $0$ the orbit is $\{0\}$ and the stabilizer is all $C_2$.
At $2$ the orbit is $\{2,-2\}$ and only the identity fixes it.
Both cases satisfy orbit–stabilizer.
6. In degree $2$, multiplication by $2$ has zero kernel, so $H_2=0$.
In degree $1$, all integers are cycles and even integers are boundaries,
so $H_1=\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z$. In degree $0$, the incoming image is zero,
so $H_0=\mathbb Z$. Other groups vanish.

## Common mistakes and misconceptions

An additive group does not imply a multiplicative group on its nonzero
elements. A subset must be closed under the stated operation. Left cosets
need not be a quotient group without normality. A vector-space theorem may
fail for modules: torsion and lack of a basis are real obstructions. “Finite
field of four elements” means $\mathbb F_4$, not arithmetic modulo $4$.
Taking point sets can discard nilpotent information from a coordinate ring.

## Applications

Symmetries of a square plate form a group because each rigid symmetry has an
inverse and composition remains a symmetry. Rotations alone form $C_4$;
including reflections gives the eight-element dihedral group. In coding,
finite-field addition and multiplication allow parity-check equations to
detect error patterns. The relevant field and error model must be specified;
integer arithmetic modulo a composite is not an automatic substitute.

## Optional computational examples

Finite operation tables can be checked exhaustively for associativity and
inverses. Polynomial division over $\mathbb F_2$ can reproduce the field
example exactly. Neither computation replaces a proof of the general finite
field classification or Galois correspondence.
